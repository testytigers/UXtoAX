Agent Experience design is a discipline that barely had a name two years ago. Now it's one of the fastest-growing specialisations in product design. But what does an AX designer actually do when they sit down at their desk?

## The core responsibility: designing agent behavior

A UX designer's primary output is usually a screen — a wireframe, a prototype, a design spec. An AX designer's primary output is a **behavior specification**: a description of how an agent should act, respond, fail, and recover across a wide range of conditions.

This requires a different way of thinking. Screens are deterministic. You design a button; it does what the button does. Agents are probabilistic. You design a behavior; the agent approximates it. Your job is to narrow the gap between what you intended and what the agent does — and to design gracefully for when that gap doesn't close.

## What AX designers build

**Conversation flow maps.** These aren't chatbot decision trees. They're probabilistic maps that show the likely paths through an interaction, with branches for ambiguity, clarification, error, and escalation. They look more like signal-processing diagrams than traditional user flows.

**Agent persona specifications.** What is the agent's name, voice, and communication style? How does it handle uncertainty — does it hedge, ask for clarification, or make a best-guess with a caveat? What is it not supposed to do? These specifications are as much a design artefact as a visual component library.

**Failure state design.** This is where AX design differs most sharply from UX. When an interface fails, you design an error message. When an agent fails, you might be designing for a scenario where the agent confidently tells a user the wrong thing. AX designers must think through hallucination scenarios, tool failures, misunderstood intent, and partial completions.

**Trust scaffolding.** Users need to know when to trust an agent and when to verify. AX designers create interaction patterns that calibrate trust without breaking task flow — things like confidence indicators, source citations, and step-by-step reasoning disclosures.

## How AX designers work differently

The job requires closer collaboration with ML engineers and AI researchers than traditional UX. You're not just handing off a Figma file — you're working iteratively with the model itself, testing prompts, evaluating outputs, and feeding findings back into the design.

You'll prototype by talking to AI models, not by building click-through flows. Your research methods will include conversation analysis alongside traditional usability testing. And you'll develop a thick tolerance for ambiguity — because agents are, by nature, ambiguous.

## What the role is not

AX design is not building chatbots. It's not writing copy for AI interfaces. And it's not "AI ethics" as a standalone function. It's the full design discipline applied to a new interaction medium — with all the rigour, research, and craft that implies.

The designers who will define this field are the ones who take it seriously as a discipline, not as a feature area. That means developing a genuine mental model of how AI agents work, building a portfolio of AX-specific work, and engaging with the research and practitioner community that is actively defining what good AX design looks like.
