Every AI product will fail its users at some point. The question is not whether, but how — and whether your design allows trust to survive the failure.

Trust in AI systems is not about making users believe the system is infallible. It's about calibrating their confidence accurately so they rely on the system appropriately and recover gracefully when it falls short.

## Trust is multi-dimensional

When users trust an AI system, they're trusting several different things simultaneously:

**Competence trust** — Does the system do what it claims to do, reliably? This is built through consistent, accurate performance over time. It's damaged by visible errors.

**Predictability trust** — Does the system behave consistently? Users develop routines with AI products. Unpredictable behavior breaks those routines and forces re-evaluation.

**Intent trust** — Does the system serve my interests, or someone else's? This is where design decisions around data use, recommendations, and nudges become trust decisions.

**Boundary trust** — Does the system stay within appropriate scope? An agent that exceeds its mandate — even helpfully — triggers alarm.

## The calibration imperative

Over-trust is as dangerous as under-trust. Users who trust an AI too much stop verifying outputs that deserve verification. They delegate decisions the system isn't qualified to make. They miss errors that compound.

Your design goal is calibrated trust: users should trust the AI exactly as much as its actual reliability warrants, in the domains where that reliability holds.

Confidence signals — progress indicators, certainty expressions, source attribution — are calibration tools. Use them deliberately.

## Designing for failure

The most important trust design work happens in edge cases: what does your system show when it doesn't know the answer? When it's operating outside its training? When a tool call fails?

Confident-but-wrong is the worst failure mode. Design your system to surface uncertainty clearly, suggest when the user should verify independently, and make it easy to report errors and try again.

Recovery flows are not error handling — they're trust-building moments. A system that handles failure gracefully earns more trust than one that never appears to fail.
